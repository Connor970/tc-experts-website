Fix and Flip Closing Checklist: From Purchase to Resale

November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Every flip has two closings, and your profit depends on both of them going smoothly. The purchase has to close on time so your rehab can start and your holding costs stay in check. The resale has to close cleanly so you actually collect the spread you worked for. Miss something on either side and the delays stack up fast.

This fix and flip closing checklist walks through what to line up at each stage, from the day you sign the purchase contract to the day the resale funds. Use it as a working list on your next deal, or hand it to your transaction coordinator and let them run it.

Why flips need their own closing checklist

A standard homebuyer checklist does not fit a flip. You are often buying with hard money or private money, purchasing in an LLC, closing fast on a distressed property, and planning a resale before you even own the house. That means more parties, more conditions and two sets of deadlines that depend on each other.

The good news is that most flip closing problems are predictable. Title issues, lender conditions, insurance, payoffs and buyer financing trip up the same deals over and over. A checklist turns those surprises into routine tasks.

Stage 1: Purchase contract to title

The first few days after you go under contract set the pace for everything that follows.

  • Send the fully signed contract to title. Include every addendum and counter. Missing initials or pages are a common reason a file sits idle.
  • Calendar every deadline. Earnest money due date, inspection or option period, financing contingency, title objection date and closing date should all live in one place.
  • Get earnest money delivered and receipted. Confirm title has it, and keep the receipt in the file.
  • Confirm the buying entity. If you buy in an LLC, make sure the contract names the right entity, or plan how you will handle it with title and your lender before closing.
  • Order the title search right away. On distressed properties, the commitment is where you find out what you are really buying.

Stage 2: Title, liens and payoffs

Flip properties are often distressed for a reason, and that usually shows up on the title commitment. Read it line by line, or have your TC do it, as soon as it arrives.

What to look for

  • Mortgages, including any second liens or HELOCs
  • Unpaid property taxes
  • Code enforcement or municipal liens
  • Judgments against the seller
  • HOA balances or HOA liens
  • Probate, divorce or missing-heir issues that affect who can sign

What to do about it

Every item on the requirements list needs an owner and a plan. That might mean requesting payoff statements, getting an HOA estoppel or status letter, or working with the seller to clear a judgment. If something looks complicated, such as an estate or a contested lien, bring in the title company or a real estate attorney early. Clearing title is far easier with weeks to spare than days.

Quick tip: Clear title on the purchase protects your resale. Anything that slips through when you buy can come back as an objection from your resale buyer's title company, right when you are trying to exit.

Stage 3: Hard money lender conditions

If you are financing the purchase or rehab, your lender will have its own list of conditions. Every lender is different, so ask for the full list on day one rather than finding out piecemeal. Common items include:

  • Entity documents such as articles of organization, operating agreement and EIN letter
  • Scope of work and rehab budget, often used to set up the draw schedule
  • Appraisal or valuation, which some lenders base on the after-repair value
  • Insurance that fits a vacant property under renovation, with the lender named as required
  • Proof of funds for your down payment, closing costs and any reserves
  • Title and closing contacts so the lender can send loan documents and wire instructions

Lender conditions are one of the biggest reasons flip purchases get pushed. Track each one, confirm when it is satisfied, and keep the lender, title and your insurance agent talking to each other instead of through you.

Stage 4: Closing day on the purchase

  • Review the settlement statement for purchase price, credits, loan amount, lender fees and any rehab holdback.
  • Confirm wire instructions by phone using a number you already have on file. Never trust wire changes sent by email.
  • Schedule signing for you and the seller, including any mobile notary or remote signing.
  • Get keys, codes and access set up for your contractor so work can start right away.
  • Save the final settlement statement, recorded deed and loan documents. You will need them at resale.

Stage 5: During the rehab

The rehab is not a closing, but what happens here decides how smooth the resale will be.

  • Request draws on time. Follow your lender's draw process and keep photos and invoices organized.
  • Pull and close permits. Open or unfinalized permits can surface during a buyer's inspection or title review. Requirements vary by city, so check with your local building department.
  • Keep insurance current. Make sure coverage matches the stage of the project and your lender's requirements.
  • Track your loan maturity date. Know when your hard money loan comes due and any extension terms, so a slow resale does not turn into a default.
  • Keep warranties and receipts. Retail buyers often ask what was replaced and when.

Stage 6: Resale contract to closing

Now you are the seller, and the buyer's timeline is the one that matters. Most retail buyers use a lender, which brings an appraisal, underwriting and their own conditions.

As soon as you accept an offer

  • Send the executed contract to title and open the file.
  • Calendar the buyer's deadlines: earnest money, inspection period, appraisal, financing and closing.
  • Request a payoff statement from your hard money lender with a good-through date past closing.
  • Confirm the selling entity matches who is on title.

While the buyer works through their contingencies

  • Repair requests. Respond quickly and in writing. Delays here eat into the buyer's other deadlines.
  • Appraisal. Have a list of upgrades and comparable sales ready for the buyer's agent in case the value comes in tight.
  • Buyer loan type. Some loan programs have rules about recently purchased homes. If your buyer is using FHA or another government-backed loan, ask their lender early whether your purchase date affects eligibility.
  • HOA documents. If the property is in an HOA, order the resale package early since turnaround times vary.

Closing day on the resale

  • Review the seller settlement statement: sale price, lender payoff, concessions, commissions and your net.
  • Confirm the payoff amount matches your lender's latest statement.
  • Confirm your proceeds wire instructions with title by phone.
  • Get the final settlement statement for your records and taxes.

Where a transaction coordinator fits on a flip

Running this checklist across one flip is manageable. Running it across several at once, while you are also finding deals and managing contractors, is where things slip. A transaction coordinator who works with investors owns the paperwork side of both closings: opening title, tracking deadlines, chasing lender conditions, ordering payoffs and HOA documents, and keeping every party updated.

If you buy your flips from wholesalers, the purchase side may look familiar. Our guide on what a transaction coordinator handles for wholesalers covers assignments and double closes. On those wholesale deals the TC fee is typically paid by the end buyer at closing, so it is usually free to the wholesaler. On your own flips, a TC is typically a flat per-file fee paid at closing, and it buys back hours you can spend on the next deal.

Frequently asked questions

What documents does a hard money lender usually need before a fix and flip closing?

Requirements vary by lender, but most ask for the signed purchase contract, your entity documents if you buy in an LLC, a scope of work and rehab budget, proof of insurance naming the lender, and title company contact details. Some also order an appraisal or valuation and want proof of funds for your down payment and closing costs.

Why do fix and flip resales fall through?

Common causes include title issues that were never fully cleared on the purchase, open permits, a low appraisal for the buyer's lender, repair requests after inspection, and delays getting a payoff statement from the investor's own lender. Most of these can be caught early with a checklist.

Can a transaction coordinator handle both closings on a flip?

Yes. A transaction coordinator who works with investors can manage the purchase side, including title, lender conditions and earnest money, and then the resale side, including the payoff, buyer deadlines and closing prep, so nothing falls between the two.

The bottom line

Flipping profits are made at the purchase and protected at the resale. A clear fix and flip closing checklist keeps both closings on schedule, keeps your holding costs down and keeps surprises off the settlement statement.
