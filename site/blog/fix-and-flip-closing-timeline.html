Fix and Flip Closing Timeline: From Contract to Resale Without Delays

November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

On a flip, time is money in the most literal way. Every extra week means another interest payment on your loan, another month of insurance, taxes and utilities, and another week before your capital is free for the next deal. Most of those weeks are not lost during the rehab. They are lost on paperwork at either end of it.

This fix and flip closing timeline breaks a flip into its two closings, the purchase and the resale, and walks through what has to happen at each stage, who is responsible, and where deals most often stall. Use it to plan your next project, or to see how much of it you could hand off.

Why a flip has two closing timelines, not one

A retail buyer closes once. A flipper closes twice on the same property, usually months apart, and each closing has a different set of players:

  • The purchase: you, the seller (or a wholesaler), your title company and, in most cases, a hard money or private lender.
  • The resale: you as the seller, your listing agent, the buyer and their agent, the buyer's lender and title, plus your own lender waiting to be paid off.

What happens on the purchase sets up the resale. A title issue you ignore when you buy, a missing permit, or a payoff you cannot get quickly will show up again when you try to sell. A good fix and flip closing timeline treats both closings as one project.

Stage 1: Under contract on the purchase

Open title the same day

Send the signed purchase agreement and every addendum to title as soon as it is executed. If you are buying from a wholesaler, include the assignment agreement and confirm the title company is comfortable closing an assigned contract. The sooner the search starts, the sooner you learn what you are really buying.

Lock in earnest money

Get your earnest money to title by the date in the contract and confirm it was receipted. Late earnest money is an easy reason for a seller to walk, especially if they have received a better offer since signing.

Start the loan right away

If you are using hard money or private money, send the contract to your lender the same day. Lenders typically need the contract, your scope of work and rehab budget, entity documents if you buy in an LLC, and an appraisal or valuation of the property. Ask your lender for their realistic turnaround and build your closing date around it, not the other way around.

Calendar every deadline

Inspection or option period, earnest money due date, financing contingency if you have one, title objection deadline and closing date. Put them in one place with reminders a few days before each.

Stage 2: Clearing title and loan conditions

Review the title commitment early

Distressed properties are distressed for a reason. Look for unpaid property taxes, code enforcement liens, judgments, mechanic's liens, HOA balances, a mortgage in default, or an estate that has not been properly handled. Each one needs a payoff, a release or a plan before closing. Finding it in week one is a negotiation. Finding it the day before closing is a delay.

Work through the lender's conditions

Your lender will issue a list of conditions to close. Common items include proof of insurance, entity documents, the title commitment, and sometimes a signed scope of work or draw schedule. Turn each one around as fast as you can and confirm the lender has marked it cleared, not just received.

Bind the right insurance

A vacant property under renovation usually needs different coverage from an occupied home. Ask your insurance agent about vacant or builder's risk coverage, and make sure the policy names your lender the way their conditions require. Have the binder in hand before closing.

Quick tip: Ask your lender for their closing conditions list on day one, not after the appraisal. Most of it, like entity documents and insurance, does not depend on the appraisal and can be cleared while you wait.

Stage 3: Closing on the purchase

Before you sign, review the settlement statement against the contract and your loan terms. Check the purchase price, lender fees and points, any rehab holdback, the payoffs of the liens title found, prorated taxes and your cash to close. Then confirm the wire instructions directly with the title company by phone using a number you already have, never from an email alone.

After closing, keep the final settlement statement, recorded deed, loan documents, draw schedule and insurance binder together. You will need several of them again when you sell.

Stage 4: During the rehab

This is not technically a closing, but what happens here decides how smoothly the resale goes.

  • Draws: track each draw request, inspection and payment so your contractors stay paid and the work keeps moving.
  • Permits: pull the permits your local building department requires and close them out with final inspections. Open permits are a common problem for buyers and their lenders at resale.
  • Lien waivers: collect lien waivers or releases from contractors and suppliers as they are paid, so nobody can file a lien against the property later.
  • Loan maturity: know your loan's maturity date and any extension terms. A rehab that runs long can push you toward an extension fee if the resale is not planned early.

Stage 5: The resale closing

Get the file ready before you list

Before the property goes on the market, gather the closed permits, warranties for new systems, your lien waivers and any disclosures your state requires sellers to complete. Buyers' agents and lenders will ask for these, and having them ready saves days.

Open title and order your payoff

Once you accept an offer, open title on the resale and request a payoff statement from your hard money lender. Many private lenders process payoffs manually, so ask early and make sure the good-through date covers your closing.

Track the buyer's side

Most retail buyers use conventional, FHA or VA financing, which brings its own appraisal, repair requests and underwriting conditions. Stay in close contact with the buyer's agent so you hear about appraisal or repair issues while there is still time to respond.

Review the seller settlement statement

Confirm the sale price, your lender payoff, commissions, any seller concessions and your net proceeds before closing day. A mistake on the payoff or a missing credit is much easier to fix before funds move than after.

Where a transaction coordinator fits

Look back through this timeline and most of it is follow-up: sending documents, chasing title and lenders, tracking dates, ordering payoffs and keeping every party updated. None of it requires you personally, but all of it has to happen on time. An investor-focused transaction coordinator handles that work across both closings, so you can spend your time on the rehab and on finding the next property.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to close on a fix and flip purchase?

It depends on how the deal is funded. Cash purchases can close as fast as title can clear the property. Hard money purchases add the lender's application, appraisal or valuation, and loan documents, so ask your lender for their typical turnaround before you agree to a closing date.

What usually delays a flip closing?

The most common delays are title issues found late, such as liens, unpaid taxes or probate problems, slow lender conditions or appraisals on the purchase, and on the resale, open permits, repair requests and the buyer's financing.

Can a transaction coordinator help with both the purchase and the resale of a flip?

Yes. An investor-focused TC can run the acquisition closing with your title company and lender, then coordinate the resale with your listing agent, the buyer's side and title, including ordering your hard money payoff.

The bottom line

A flip makes its money in the spread, but it loses money in the waiting. Treat the purchase and the resale as one fix and flip closing timeline, open title and the loan the day you sign, clear permits and lien waivers as the rehab moves, and order your payoff the moment the resale goes under contract. Run every flip that way, or hand the timeline to a TC who manages it every day, and you will spend fewer weeks paying interest on paperwork.
