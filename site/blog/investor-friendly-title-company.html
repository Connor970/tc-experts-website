Investor-Friendly Title Company: How Wholesalers Find One and What to Ask

November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

You can find a great deal, negotiate a strong price and line up a cash buyer, and still watch the whole thing stall because the title company does not want to close it. For wholesalers, an investor-friendly title company is not a nice-to-have. It is the difference between collecting your assignment fee on schedule and scrambling for a new closer the week of closing.

Here is what "investor-friendly" actually means, how to find one in your market, the questions to ask before you send a contract, and the red flags that tell you to keep looking.

What is an investor-friendly title company?

Every title company searches title, issues title insurance and closes transactions. What separates an investor-friendly title company is experience with the deal structures wholesalers use every day and a willingness to close them.

In practice, that usually means a title company that:

  • Closes assignments of contract and knows how to show your assignment fee on the settlement statement.
  • Handles double closings, including back-to-back A-to-B and B-to-C closings on the same day.
  • Works with transactional funding and hard-money lenders that investors and end buyers rely on.
  • Moves at investor speed. Cash buyers often want to close in days or a couple of weeks, not 30 to 45.
  • Communicates proactively about title issues like liens, judgments, probate or HOA balances instead of letting them surface at the last minute.

A retail-focused title company can be excellent at what it does and still be the wrong fit for a wholesale file. The goal is to match your deal to a closer who sees deals like it all the time.

Why some title companies will not close wholesale deals

If you have ever had a title company back out after you sent the contract, it is rarely personal. Common reasons include:

  • Company or underwriter policy. Title companies issue policies on behalf of underwriters, and some have internal guidelines around contract assignments or back-to-back closings.
  • How funds move on a double close. Some closers will not use the end buyer's funds to fund the first leg of a double close, which is why transactional funding exists.
  • State rules. Several states have added rules that affect wholesaling, such as disclosure or licensing requirements. These vary and change, so check with a local real estate attorney or your title company on what applies where you buy.
  • Unfamiliarity. A closer who rarely sees assignments may simply be cautious with a structure they do not know well.

The fix is not to argue. It is to vet title companies before you need them, so every contract you sign already has a closer lined up.

How to find an investor-friendly title company in your market

Ask active investors and buyers

The fastest route is to ask people who close investor deals locally. Your cash buyers, other wholesalers, flippers and hard-money lenders all have closers they trust. If the same name comes up more than once, start there.

Go to local investor meetups

Title reps who want investor business often attend or sponsor local real estate investor association meetings. That alone tells you they are open to your deals.

Ask your lenders

Hard-money and transactional lenders close with title companies all day. They know which ones are responsive and which ones slow files down.

Ask your transaction coordinator

A TC who works wholesale files sees how different title companies handle assignments, double closes, payoffs and HOA requests. That firsthand experience is often the quickest shortcut to a reliable closer.

Call and interview before you send a contract

Once you have a short list, call each one. Ask for an escrow officer or closer who handles investor transactions, then work through the questions below.

Questions to ask a title company before you send a wholesale contract

  1. Do you close assignments of contract? If yes, ask how they want the assignment agreement and fee documented.
  2. Do you close double closings? Ask whether they can close both legs on the same day and what they need from you to do it.
  3. Which transactional lenders do you work with? If you plan to double close, you want a closer who is comfortable with the funding you will use.
  4. How fast can you turn a title commitment? Cash buyers want quick closings, and the commitment is often the first bottleneck.
  5. Who is my day-to-day contact? One named closer or processor beats a general inbox.
  6. How do you handle curative work? Ask how they approach liens, judgments, missing heirs and payoff requests, and how quickly they flag problems.
  7. Do you offer mobile or remote signings? Out-of-state sellers and buyers are common on investor deals.
  8. Who pays for what? Confirm how they typically split title and closing costs on investor deals in your area, then make sure your contract matches.

Quick tip: Ask every title company the same questions and write down the answers. When a deal is on the clock, you will know exactly which closer to use for an assignment, which one to use for a double close, and who to call when something goes sideways.

Red flags to watch for

  • Vague answers about whether they close assignments or double closes.
  • No single point of contact. Your file gets passed around and nobody owns it.
  • Slow responses during vetting. If they take days to call back before you are a client, expect the same on a live file.
  • Surprises late in the process. Liens or requirements that should have come up on the commitment but only show up days before closing.
  • Discomfort with your fee. A closer who seems surprised by an assignment fee on the settlement statement probably does not see many wholesale deals.

Assignments vs. double closes: matching the deal to the closer

Not every title company handles both structures equally well. Some wholesalers keep one closer for clean assignments and another for double closes that involve transactional funding. That is a smart setup.

Where a transaction coordinator fits in

Finding a great title company is step one. Keeping the file moving is step two, and that is where most wholesalers lose time. A TC:

  • Opens title and sends the contract and assignment the same day you go under contract.
  • Tracks earnest money, inspection deadlines and the closing date.
  • Follows up on the title commitment, payoffs and HOA documents.
  • Reviews the settlement statement to make sure your fee is correct.
  • Keeps the seller, end buyer and title company updated so you are not fielding status calls.

The best part for wholesalers: on most wholesale deals, the TC fee is paid by the end buyer at closing. That means having a coordinator manage your title company relationship is typically free to you as the wholesaler.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a title company investor-friendly?

An investor-friendly title company regularly closes assignments of contract and double closings, understands how wholesale fees appear on the settlement statement, works with transactional and hard-money lenders, and moves fast enough for cash-buyer timelines.

Why would a title company refuse to close a wholesale deal?

Common reasons include internal or underwriter policies on assignments and back-to-back closings, concerns about how funds move on a double close, state rules that affect wholesaling, or simply unfamiliarity with investor deals. Ask early so you are not surprised after the contract is signed.

Can my transaction coordinator choose the title company?

A TC who works investor deals usually knows which local title companies close assignments and double closes smoothly and can recommend one. You still decide, but a TC can open title, send the contract and keep the file moving the same day.

The bottom line

Your title company is one of the most important members of your wholesaling team. Vet closers before you need them, ask direct questions about assignments and double closes, and keep a short list of investor-friendly title companies you trust. Then pair that closer with a transaction coordinator who keeps every deadline and document on track, so your next assignment fee shows up on schedule.
