Seller Financing Closing Process: How Investors Close Owner-Financed Deals

November 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Seller financing can be the best tool in a creative-finance investor's kit. No bank, no underwriting, flexible terms and a seller who becomes your lender. But "no bank" does not mean "no paperwork." The seller financing closing process has its own documents, its own deadlines and its own ways to go sideways, and the mistakes made at closing can follow you for the life of the note.

This guide walks through how an owner-financed deal gets from handshake to recorded deed, what has to be in place at each step, and where a transaction coordinator keeps things moving.

What makes a seller financed closing different

In a traditional purchase, the lender drives much of the closing. It orders the appraisal, sets the conditions, sends the loan package and wires the money. In a seller-financed deal, the seller is the lender, and most sellers have never done this before. That means someone else has to make sure the loan side of the deal is documented, signed and recorded correctly.

Instead of a bank loan package, you will typically see:

  • A purchase agreement with seller financing terms, or a financing addendum spelling them out.
  • A promissory note, the buyer's promise to repay: amount, interest rate, payment schedule, maturity date, late fees and any balloon.
  • A security instrument, usually a deed of trust or a mortgage depending on the state, that ties the note to the property.
  • A deed transferring ownership to the buyer at closing.

Some deals use other structures, like a contract for deed, a lease-option or a wraparound note over an existing loan. Each comes with different rules and risks, so confirm the structure with your attorney before documents are drafted.

The seller financing closing process, step by step

1. Lock down the terms in writing

Before anyone drafts a note, every term should be agreed and written into the contract: price, down payment, amount financed, interest rate, amortization, monthly payment, balloon date, prepayment rights, late charges and who pays taxes and insurance. Vague terms are the number one reason seller-finance deals stall at the closing table. If the seller and buyer remember the deal differently, the documents cannot be finished.

2. Open title and order the search

Send the signed contract to an investor-friendly title company or closing attorney right away. Title searches the property for existing mortgages, liens, judgments, unpaid taxes and HOA balances. This matters even more in seller financing, because the seller is carrying paper. A buyer does not want to pay a seller for years only to learn there was an old lien on the house, and a seller wants their lien position to be clear.

3. Check the existing loan, if there is one

If the seller still has a mortgage, you need to know about it now. Either the loan is paid off at closing, or the deal becomes a wrap or a subject-to structure, which carries due-on-sale risk and needs careful handling. Get the payoff or loan details early so there are no surprises on the settlement statement.

4. Handle compliance before documents are drafted

Seller financing can fall under federal and state lending rules, especially when the property will be the buyer's primary residence. Rules such as those under the Dodd-Frank Act and the SAFE Act can affect who may originate the loan and what terms are allowed, and some sellers use a licensed residential mortgage loan originator to review the deal. Requirements vary by situation and by state, so have a real estate attorney confirm what applies before you finalize terms.

5. Draft the note and security instrument

The attorney or title company drafts the promissory note, the deed of trust or mortgage, and the deed. Both parties should review drafts before closing day. Check that the numbers in the note match the contract exactly, including the payment amount, first payment date and balloon date.

6. Set up insurance and taxes

The buyer needs a homeowner's or landlord policy in place by closing, with the seller listed as mortgagee or loss payee. Decide whether property taxes and insurance will be paid directly by the buyer or through an escrow arrangement, and write that into the documents.

7. Arrange loan servicing

Who collects payments, tracks the balance and sends year-end statements? Many investors use a third-party loan servicer so payments are documented and both sides have a neutral record. It also makes the note easier to sell later if the seller ever wants to cash out.

8. Review the settlement statement and close

The settlement statement should show the down payment, the seller-carried amount as a credit to the buyer, any payoff of existing liens, prorations and closing costs. Once everyone signs, title collects funds, disburses to the seller and moves on to recording.

9. Record everything and confirm

The deed and the deed of trust or mortgage get recorded with the county. Recording protects the buyer's ownership and the seller's lien. Follow up to confirm recording, get the recorded copies to both parties, and send the servicer what it needs to board the loan.

Quick tip: Put the first payment date, balloon date and insurance renewal date on a shared calendar the day you close. Missed dates after closing cause as many seller-finance headaches as anything that happens before it.

How a transaction coordinator helps on owner-financed deals

Seller financing puts more moving parts on the investor. You are negotiating terms, keeping the seller comfortable, managing title, coordinating document prep, setting up servicing and tracking deadlines, often while working other deals at the same time. Seller financing closing checklist

  1. Signed contract or addendum with all financing terms filled in.
  2. Title opened and search ordered.
  3. Existing loan identified and payoff or structure confirmed.
  4. Attorney review of compliance and structure.
  5. Note, security instrument and deed drafted and reviewed by both sides.
  6. Insurance binder with the seller named as mortgagee or loss payee.
  7. Tax and insurance payment method agreed.
  8. Loan servicer selected, or self-servicing plan documented.
  9. Settlement statement reviewed against the contract.
  10. Deed and security instrument recorded, copies sent to all parties.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a title company to close a seller financed deal?

It is not always required, but it is strongly recommended. A title company or closing attorney searches title, issues title insurance, handles the money and records the deed and security instrument, which protects both the buyer and the seller.

How long does a seller financing closing take?

Because there is no bank underwriting or appraisal requirement, seller-financed deals can often close faster than a traditional financed purchase. The real timeline depends on how quickly terms are agreed, title is cleared and the loan documents are drafted and approved by both sides.

Who prepares the promissory note and deed of trust in a seller financed sale?

Usually a real estate attorney, or the title company if it offers document preparation. Using professionally drafted documents that fit your state is important, because these documents control the loan for its entire life.

The bottom line

Seller financing gives investors room to structure deals that a bank never would, but the closing has to be just as tight as a conventional one, and in some ways tighter, because the relationship lasts for years. Lock the terms in writing, open title early, get the documents drafted and reviewed by a professional, and confirm everything is recorded.
