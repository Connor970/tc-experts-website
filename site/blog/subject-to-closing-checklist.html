Subject-To Closing Checklist: How to Close a Subject-To Deal Step by Step

November 25, 2026 · 7 min read

Getting a seller to agree to subject-to terms is the hard part. Closing the deal cleanly is where most creative-finance investors actually get hurt. A missed mortgage statement, a lapsed insurance policy or a title company that backs out the week of closing can turn a great deal into a mess. This subject-to closing checklist walks through what to gather, verify and set up before, during and after closing, so the existing loan keeps getting paid and nothing slips through the cracks.

Use it as a working list on every file. Every state and every deal is a little different, so treat it as a starting point and confirm the specifics with your title company or real estate attorney.

Why a subject-to deal needs its own closing checklist

In a subject-to purchase, you take title to the property while the seller's existing mortgage stays in place and in the seller's name. You make the payments going forward. That structure creates moving parts a normal cash or financed closing does not have:

  • The loan does not get paid off at closing, so you need accurate, current loan information instead of a simple payoff.
  • The seller stays on the hook for the loan, which means disclosures and clear documentation matter more than usual.
  • Most mortgages include a due-on-sale clause, which generally gives the lender the right to call the loan due when the property is transferred. Your seller needs to understand that risk in writing.
  • Insurance, escrow and payments all have to be handed off without a gap.

A standard closing checklist will not catch these. A subject-to checklist will.

Phase 1: Before you sign the contract

Confirm the loan details

Get the seller's most recent mortgage statement for every loan on the property, including any second mortgage or HELOC. Note the servicer, loan number, unpaid balance, interest rate, monthly payment, escrow amount and whether the loan is current. If the seller is behind, you need the reinstatement amount, not just the monthly payment.

Line up a closer who handles subject-to

Not every title company or closing attorney will close a transfer where the existing loan stays in place. Some have internal or underwriter policies against it. Call first and ask directly. Our guide to finding an investor-friendly title company has the questions to ask, and they apply even more on creative-finance files.

Check your state's rules

Some states regulate certain creative-finance structures, especially ones involving owner-occupied homes, distressed sellers or contracts for deed. These rules vary and change. Before you build your contract, talk to a local real estate attorney about what applies where you are buying.

Phase 2: Under contract, before closing

Collect the loan authorization

Have the seller sign an authorization that allows the lender to release loan information to you, your TC and the closer. Without it, the servicer will not talk to anyone but the borrower, and you will be chasing the seller for every answer.

Order a loan status or payoff statement

Even though the loan is not being paid off, the closer will usually want an official statement from the servicer showing the balance, payment status and any amounts past due. Order it early. Servicers can be slow, and the statement has an expiration date.

Open title and review the commitment

Title still needs to be searched. Watch for:

  • Second mortgages or HELOCs the seller did not mention
  • Judgments, tax liens or code enforcement liens
  • Unpaid property taxes, especially if the loan has no escrow account
  • HOA balances or violations
  • Probate, divorce or ownership issues that affect who has to sign

The existing mortgage will typically show up as an exception on your title policy because it is not being released. Make sure you understand what your policy covers and what it does not.

Prepare seller disclosures

The seller should sign clear disclosures that the loan stays in their name, that the lender may have the right to call the loan due because of the transfer, that their credit is tied to the payments being made, and how payments will be handled. Have your attorney draft or review these. Clear disclosures protect the seller and protect you.

Plan the insurance handoff

The seller's homeowner policy usually will not cover a property they no longer own or live in. Talk to an insurance agent who works with investors about the right policy, how the lender should be listed as mortgagee, and whether the seller should be named on it. The goal is no coverage gap on closing day and no surprise force-placed insurance from the lender later.

Decide how payments will be made

Decide before closing who sends the payment, from which account, and how the seller can verify it was made. Many investors use a third-party loan servicing company so payment records are documented and both parties can see them. Whatever you choose, put it in writing.

Phase 3: Closing day

  • Review the settlement statement line by line. Confirm the purchase price, any cash to the seller, arrears being brought current, prorated taxes and your closing costs.
  • Confirm arrears are paid at closing if the loan was behind, and get proof the funds went to the servicer.
  • Sign and collect all documents: deed, disclosures, loan authorization, any power of attorney or servicing agreement your attorney requires.
  • Get the seller's loan login, statements and escrow details so you can monitor the account going forward.
  • Collect keys, garage openers, codes and utility information before the seller leaves.

Quick tip: Ask the closer to send you the recorded deed and final settlement statement as soon as they are available. Store them with the seller disclosures and loan authorization in one file. If anyone ever has a question about the deal, everything is in one place.

Phase 4: After closing

Subject-to deals do not end at the closing table. The loan stays active for years, so the post-closing list matters as much as anything above.

  1. Confirm the deed recorded and save the recorded copy.
  2. Make the first payment on time and confirm it posted. Set up reminders or autopay for every payment after that.
  3. Confirm the new insurance is active and that the lender has the updated policy information.
  4. Watch the escrow account. If taxes or insurance change, the monthly payment will change too. Review the annual escrow statement.
  5. Transfer utilities and update the mailing address for tax bills where your state allows.
  6. Keep the seller informed. A simple payment confirmation each month builds trust and heads off nervous phone calls.

Common mistakes that derail subject-to closings

  • Relying on an old mortgage statement. Balances, payments and late fees change. Always get current numbers.
  • Missing a second lien. A forgotten HELOC can blow up your numbers at the last minute.
  • Waiting too long to find a closer. Finding out the title company will not close subject-to after you have signed the contract costs you days you do not have.
  • Thin seller disclosures. If the seller does not clearly understand the structure in writing, you are creating risk for both of you.
  • An insurance gap. Canceling the seller's policy before yours is active leaves the property exposed.
  • No payment system. Payments made "manually when I remember" are how subject-to deals go wrong months after closing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a title company close a subject-to deal?

Many can, but not all will. Some title companies and underwriters have policies against closing transfers where the existing loan stays in place. Ask before you sign the contract, and work with a closer or real estate attorney who regularly handles creative-finance deals.

What documents do I need to close a subject-to deal?

Typically the purchase agreement with subject-to terms, the seller's recent mortgage statements, a signed authorization to release loan information, seller disclosures acknowledging the loan stays in their name, a payoff or loan status letter, insurance documents and the deed. Your attorney or title company may require more depending on your state.

Who makes the mortgage payment after a subject-to closing?

The buyer takes over making the payments on the existing loan, but the loan stays in the seller's name. Many investors use a third-party loan servicing company or a documented payment process so both sides can see that every payment is made on time.

The bottom line

A subject-to deal can be one of the best tools in a creative-finance investor's kit, but only if it closes cleanly and stays clean afterward. Verify the loan, line up the right closer, document everything with the seller, close the insurance gap and set up a payment system before you sign.
