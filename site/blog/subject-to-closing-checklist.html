Subject-To Closing Checklist: How to Close a Subject-To Deal Without Surprises

November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

A subject-to deal can be one of the best acquisitions in your portfolio. You take title, the seller's existing loan stays in place, and you skip new financing. But the closing has more moving parts than a cash purchase, and the deal does not end at the closing table. The loan stays active in the seller's name, so every detail you skip now can turn into a problem months later.

This subject-to closing checklist walks through what to verify, collect and set up at each stage, from signed contract to the first payment after closing. Use it to run your own files, or to judge whether a transaction coordinator knows creative-finance deals as well as they say.

Why you need a subject-to closing checklist

A standard closing checklist assumes the seller's loan gets paid off. On a subject-to deal, it does not. That changes almost everything about how the file is managed:

  • The existing loan is the deal. Its balance, rate, payment, escrow and status all have to be verified, not assumed.
  • The seller stays involved. You need their cooperation for authorizations, documents and sometimes questions after closing.
  • Not every title company will close it. Some decline subject-to files or have extra requirements.
  • The work continues after closing. Payments, insurance and servicer access all need a clean handoff.

Most subject-to deals that go sideways do not fail because of the structure. They fail because a detail was missed: arrears nobody confirmed, an insurance policy that lapsed, or a payment that went to the wrong place.

Stage 1: Before you open title

1. Get a recent mortgage statement for every loan

Ask the seller for the most recent statement on each loan against the property, including any second mortgage or HELOC. You want the servicer name, loan number, principal balance, monthly payment broken into principal, interest and escrow, and any past-due amount. If the seller cannot find a statement, that is your first signal to slow down and verify directly with the servicer.

2. Collect a borrower authorization

Servicers generally will not discuss a loan with anyone but the borrower unless the borrower authorizes it. Have the seller sign the servicer's third-party authorization form, or a general authorization to release information, so you or your TC can confirm the balance, payment history and status. Expect some servicers to insist on their own form.

3. Confirm the loan status and any arrears

Many subject-to sellers are behind on payments. Get the exact reinstatement amount, the good-through date, and whether the loan has been referred to a foreclosure attorney or trustee. If a sale date is set, your timeline just got much shorter, and every party needs to know it on day one.

4. Pick a title company or attorney that closes subject-to deals

Call before you send the contract. Ask whether they close subject-to transactions, what documents and disclosures they require, how they handle reinstatement funds, and whether they will issue an owner's title policy. A five-minute call here saves a week of back-and-forth later.

Quick tip: Disclosure and contract requirements for subject-to and other creative-finance deals vary by state, and some states have specific rules for these transactions. Your title company or a real estate attorney in that state is the right source for what applies to your deal.

Stage 2: Under contract and in escrow

5. Open title and order a full search

Send the signed purchase agreement and any addenda to title the same day. The search should surface every recorded lien: the existing mortgage, any junior liens, tax liens, judgments, code liens and HOA liens. On a subject-to deal you are keeping the first mortgage, but almost everything else usually has to be paid, released or negotiated before closing.

6. Check taxes, HOA and utilities

If the loan does not escrow taxes, confirm what is owed and what is coming due. If there is an HOA, request a payoff or status letter and confirm any transfer requirements. Note the utility providers so service can move over at closing without a gap.

7. Line up insurance before closing day

Insurance is one of the most overlooked items on a subject-to file. The existing policy is in the seller's name and was written for an owner-occupant. Talk with an insurance agent who understands investor and subject-to purchases about the right coverage, how the lender should be listed, and how to keep the lender's insurance requirements satisfied. Have the new policy bound before you close, not after.

8. Track every deadline in one place

Inspection or due diligence period, earnest money due date, title objection deadline, reinstatement good-through date and closing date. On a pre-foreclosure file, add the sale date in bold. One calendar, with reminders before each date, is the difference between a smooth file and a scramble.

Stage 3: Closing day

9. Review the settlement statement line by line

Because the existing loan is not being paid off, the settlement statement looks different from a standard sale. Confirm that the loan is shown as taken subject to rather than paid off, that any reinstatement amount goes to the servicer, that junior liens and taxes are handled, and that the cash to the seller matches what you agreed. Catch errors before the signing appointment, not at it.

10. Confirm everything the seller signs

Beyond the deed, make sure the seller signs every authorization and disclosure you and your title company agreed on. Collect any keys, codes, garage openers and HOA access items. Get the seller's updated contact information, because you may need it after closing.

Stage 4: After closing

11. Set up the payment method and confirm the first due date

Decide exactly how the mortgage will be paid going forward, whether directly to the servicer or through a loan servicing company, and confirm the first payment that is now your responsibility. Do not assume the seller's autopay is still running or that it should be. A missed payment in the first month after closing is avoidable.

12. Hand off a clean file

Store the recorded deed, final settlement statement, title policy, insurance declarations, borrower authorization, servicer contact details and loan number together. If you work with a property manager or loan servicer, they need this information on day one.

Frequently asked questions

What documents do I need to close a subject-to deal?

At minimum: the signed purchase agreement with subject-to terms, a recent mortgage statement, a borrower authorization so you can speak with the servicer, any disclosures your state or title company requires, the deed, and proof of a new insurance policy. Your title company or attorney will tell you what else your state requires.

Can a title company close a subject-to deal?

Many can, but not all will. Confirm before you open the file that the title company closes subject-to transactions, what documents it needs, and whether it will issue a title policy. Some investors use a real estate attorney instead.

Does a transaction coordinator help after a subject-to closing?

A good one does. A subject-to deal leaves an active loan in the seller's name, so the handoff matters: confirming the payment method, servicer access, insurance and the first payment due date so nothing slips in the first months after closing.

The bottom line

Subject-to deals reward investors who are organized. The structure is not the hard part. The details are: verifying the loan, collecting the right authorizations, handling insurance and liens, and setting up payments so the loan stays current. Work through this checklist on every file, or hand it to a TC who already runs it daily, and you will close creative-finance deals with fewer surprises and more time for the next one.
