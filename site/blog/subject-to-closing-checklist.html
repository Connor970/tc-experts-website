Subject-To Closing Checklist: How to Close a Sub-To Deal Without Surprises

November 6, 2026 · 8 min read

Getting a seller to agree to a subject-to deal is the hard part. Or so it seems until you try to close it. Suddenly you are dealing with a loan you did not originate, a servicer that will not talk to you, a title company that has questions, and an insurance policy that still lists the seller as the owner. Miss one piece and you can end up owning a house with a payment you cannot see or verify.

This subject-to closing checklist walks through every step, from the day the seller signs to the first payment you make after closing. Use it on your next deal, or hand it to your transaction coordinator and let them run it.

Why subject-to deals need their own closing checklist

On a normal purchase, the seller's loan gets paid off at closing and the buyer brings new financing. On a subject-to deal, the existing loan stays in place. You take title to the property while the seller's mortgage keeps running, and you take over making the payments.

That one difference changes almost everything about the closing. Title still has to be clean, but the main lien is not being paid off. The payoff letter you would normally order is replaced by a loan verification. Insurance, payment setup and records all need extra attention. A standard retail closing checklist simply does not cover it.

It also means you are relying on a loan that, in most cases, includes a due-on-sale clause. That clause gives the lender the right to call the loan due when the property is transferred. Experienced creative-finance investors go in knowing this risk and plan for it. A clean, well-documented closing will not remove that risk, but it puts you in the strongest position to manage it.

Phase 1: Before you open title

Verify the existing loan

Do not rely on what the seller remembers. Get a recent mortgage statement for every loan on the property and confirm the servicer, loan number, principal balance, interest rate, monthly payment, escrow amount and whether the loan is current or behind.

Get a signed borrower authorization

Have the seller sign an authorization that allows you, your TC and the title company to speak with the servicer about the loan. Without it, the servicer will usually refuse to share anything, and you will be guessing at the numbers.

Check for arrears and foreclosure activity

If the seller is behind, find out exactly how much it takes to bring the loan current. Request a reinstatement quote from the servicer and check the county records for any notice of default or scheduled sale. A sale date changes your entire timeline.

Confirm a title company that closes subject-to

Not every title company or underwriter will close a subject-to transaction or insure it. Ask before you open the file. You want a title officer who has done these before and knows what their underwriter will require.

Phase 2: Under contract and in escrow

Open title and send the full package

Send the purchase agreement, all addenda and any subject-to specific documents your attorney has prepared. Tell the title officer up front that the existing loan will remain in place so the file is set up correctly from day one.

Review the title commitment line by line

Look for every lien besides the mortgage you are taking over: second mortgages, HELOCs, property tax balances, judgments, code liens, mechanic's liens and HOA balances. These usually need to be paid or resolved at closing even though the first mortgage is not. If a second lien exists, decide early whether you are paying it off or taking it subject-to as well.

Collect HOA information

If there is an HOA, order the payoff or resale certificate and confirm dues, any special assessments and any transfer fees. Note the HOA's contact details for your records after closing.

Track every deadline

Inspection or option periods, earnest money due dates, any servicer quote expiration and the closing date all go on one calendar. Reinstatement quotes in particular expire, so line up their timing with your closing date.

Handle disclosures and state requirements

Rules for creative-finance transactions vary by state, and some states have specific disclosure or contract requirements. This is not an area to guess. Have a local real estate attorney review your documents and tell you exactly what your state requires before closing.

Quick tip: Keep a single file for each subject-to deal that holds the mortgage statement, borrower authorization, reinstatement quote, title commitment and insurance documents. When something comes up two years later, you will be glad you can find all of it in one place.

Phase 3: Insurance and payment setup

Get insurance right before closing

The seller's homeowners policy was written for an owner-occupant. Once you take title, coverage needs to reflect the new ownership and use, and the lender still needs to be listed as mortgagee. Investors handle this in a few different ways, so work with an insurance agent who understands subject-to purchases. Have proof of the new coverage in hand before closing day, not after.

Decide how payments will be made

Before closing, decide how the monthly payment will be made and who will make it: you directly, or a third-party loan servicing company. Many investors prefer a servicing company because it creates a clean record of every payment, which protects both you and the seller.

Plan for the escrow account

If the loan has an escrow account for taxes and insurance, discuss with your attorney and title officer how the existing escrow balance will be handled. Make sure your payment amount accounts for future escrow changes.

Phase 4: Closing day

  • Review the settlement statement. Confirm the purchase price, any arrears being paid to reinstate the loan, other lien payoffs, HOA items, your closing costs and any cash to the seller.
  • Confirm signing appointments. Make sure the seller, you and any notary or attorney are scheduled and know what documents they are signing.
  • Get copies of everything. Every signed document, plus the final settlement statement, goes into your deal file.
  • Confirm reinstatement funds are wired. If you are bringing the loan current, verify the servicer received the funds and applied them.

Phase 5: After closing

This is where many subject-to deals quietly go wrong. Closing is not the finish line. It is the start of a loan you are now responsible for.

  1. Confirm the deed recorded. Get a copy of the recorded deed from title or the county.
  2. Make the first payment on time. Set up the payment through your servicing company or account before the next due date.
  3. Set up access to the loan. With the seller's authorization, set up a way to see monthly statements so you can confirm each payment posts.
  4. Update utilities, HOA and tax mailing addresses. Notices for the property should come to you, not the seller's old address.
  5. Calendar the annual checks. Insurance renewal, escrow analysis and property tax due dates all need a reminder.

Frequently asked questions

Will any title company close a subject-to deal?

No. Some title companies and underwriters decline subject-to transactions or will only close them with extra conditions. Confirm before you open the file that the title company has closed subject-to deals and will issue an owner's policy on yours.

What happens to the seller's homeowners insurance on a subject-to deal?

The coverage needs to match the new ownership, and the lender still needs to be listed as mortgagee. Investors handle this in different ways, so work with an insurance agent who understands subject-to purchases before closing rather than after.

Can a transaction coordinator handle a subject-to closing?

Yes, if they have investor and creative-finance experience. A TC can verify the loan, coordinate with title, collect the authorization and insurance documents, track deadlines and set up the post-closing payment handoff, while your attorney handles legal documents and advice.

The bottom line

Subject-to deals can be some of the most profitable in creative finance, but only when the closing is handled with care. Verify the loan, pick a title company that knows these deals, get insurance and payments set up before closing day, and keep tracking the loan long after you get the keys. Run every deal through this subject-to closing checklist, or let a TC who does it every day run it for you, and you will avoid the surprises that sink so many sub-to deals.
