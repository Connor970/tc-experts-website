Subject-To Closing Checklist: How to Close a Sub2 Deal Without Loose Ends

November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

A subject-to deal can look simple on paper: the seller deeds you the property, the existing mortgage stays in place, and you take over the payments. In practice, there are more moving parts than a cash close, and most of them live outside the closing table. A solid subject-to closing checklist keeps you from discovering a missed payment, a lapsed insurance policy or a surprise lien after the deed is already recorded.

This guide walks through what to verify before closing, what should be signed at closing, and what has to happen afterward so the deal keeps performing for you and for the seller.

Why subject-to deals need their own closing checklist

On a standard purchase, the old loan gets paid off and a clean title policy wraps things up. On a subject-to deal, the seller's loan stays open in the seller's name. That changes what you need to check:

  • The loan becomes your responsibility in practice, even though it is not in your name. You need accurate numbers and access to the account.
  • The seller is trusting you with their credit. Clear documentation and reliable payments protect both of you.
  • Not every closer handles it. Some title companies and underwriters will not close or insure subject-to transfers, so you have to line up the right partner early.
  • Most mortgages include a due-on-sale clause. That clause generally gives the lender the right to call the loan due if the property is transferred. Talk through that risk with your seller and get advice from a real estate attorney on how to structure and disclose it in your state.

None of that makes subject-to a bad strategy. It just means the paperwork and follow-through matter more than on a typical flip or wholesale close.

Before closing: verify the loan and the property

1. Get current mortgage statements

Ask the seller for the most recent mortgage statement for every loan on the property, including any second mortgage or HELOC. You want the lender name, loan number, current principal balance, interest rate, monthly payment, escrow amount and the next due date.

2. Get a signed borrower authorization

Have the seller sign an authorization that lets you, your TC or your title company speak with the lender about the loan. Lenders usually will not share account details without one. Call the servicer to confirm the balance, payment amount and whether the loan is current.

3. Confirm any arrears and reinstatement amount

Many subject-to sellers are behind on payments. If so, request a written reinstatement quote from the servicer and confirm how long it is good for. That number goes into your closing math and onto the settlement statement if the arrears are being cured at closing.

4. Check for foreclosure activity

If the loan is delinquent, find out whether a foreclosure has been started and whether a sale date is set. Your closing timeline has to beat that date with room to spare, and the reinstatement must actually reach the servicer in time.

5. Review escrow for taxes and insurance

Confirm whether taxes and insurance are paid through the loan's escrow account or directly by the owner. If they are paid directly, check that property taxes are current and find out when the next bill is due.

6. Order title and review the commitment

Open title as soon as you are under contract. Look for other liens, judgments, unpaid taxes, HOA balances, code violations and anything else that will survive the transfer. Subject-to does not make those problems go away. They become yours.

7. Check HOA requirements

If the property is in an HOA, request a payoff or resale certificate and confirm any transfer fees, dues balances and rules on rentals if you plan to rent the property out.

Line up the right closer early

Before you send the contract anywhere, call your title company or closing attorney and ask directly: do you close subject-to transactions, and will you issue an owner's title policy on one? Some will, some will not, and some have conditions. Getting a clear answer up front saves days.

The same vetting approach we cover in our guide to finding an investor-friendly title company applies here. Ask about creative-finance experience specifically, not just assignments and double closes.

Quick tip: Ask your closer what documents they want from you and the seller before they will schedule signing. Getting their list early lets your TC chase everything in parallel instead of one item at a time.

At closing: what should be signed

The exact document package depends on your state and your closer, so have a title company or real estate attorney prepare or review it. Common items on a subject-to closing include:

  • Purchase agreement or addendum spelling out the subject-to terms, who pays what, and how the existing loan will be handled.
  • Deed transferring the property to you or your entity.
  • Borrower authorization so you and your servicer can access the loan account going forward.
  • Seller disclosures and acknowledgments explaining that the loan stays in the seller's name, the due-on-sale risk, and how payments will be made.
  • Settlement statement showing any cash to the seller, arrears cured, closing costs and prorations.
  • Insurance documents showing the new coverage is in place on the day you take ownership.

Before signing day, review the settlement statement line by line. Confirm that reinstatement funds, if any, are going to the right servicer and that your numbers match the contract.

Insurance: the step investors most often get wrong

Insurance on a subject-to deal deserves its own line on your checklist. The seller's existing homeowner's policy may not fit once they no longer own or live in the property, and the lender typically requires continuous coverage with the lender listed as mortgagee.

Work with an insurance agent who understands creative-finance transfers. Tell them exactly how the deal is structured, ask how the seller and lender should be listed on the new policy, and make sure the lender receives proof of coverage. Do not let the old policy lapse before the new one is active.

After closing: set up servicing and tracking

A subject-to deal does not end at the closing table. This is where a checklist pays for itself.

Set up how payments will be made

Decide whether you will pay the lender directly or use a third-party loan servicer. Many investors prefer a servicer because it creates a clear payment record the seller can see. Either way, set the payment to land well before the due date, not on it.

Record and store everything

Confirm the deed was recorded and keep a copy. Store the closing package, authorizations, insurance declarations, and lender contact information in one file you can find in a minute.

Calendar every recurring obligation

  • Monthly mortgage payment for each loan on the property
  • Insurance renewal dates
  • Property tax due dates, if not escrowed
  • HOA dues, if any
  • Annual escrow analysis from the lender, which can change the monthly payment

Keep the seller informed

Give the seller a way to confirm payments are being made. A short update or access to a servicer portal goes a long way toward trust, and it protects you if questions come up later.

Where a transaction coordinator fits in

Subject-to deals reward investors who are organized. A transaction coordinator who works creative-finance files can:

  • Collect mortgage statements and borrower authorizations from the seller.
  • Call servicers to verify balances and request reinstatement quotes.
  • Open title, track the commitment and follow up on liens, HOA and tax items.
  • Coordinate with your closer, insurance agent and servicer so nothing is waiting on someone else.
  • Review the settlement statement against your contract before signing day.
  • Build the post-closing calendar so payments, renewals and tax dates are covered.

Frequently asked questions

Can a title company close a subject-to deal?

Many can, but not all will. Some title companies or their underwriters are not comfortable insuring a transfer where the existing loan stays in place. Ask before you send the contract, and work with a closer who regularly handles creative-finance deals. Some investors also use a real estate attorney to close.

What documents does a seller sign on a subject-to deal?

Typically the purchase agreement with subject-to terms, the deed, an authorization that lets you or your servicer speak with the lender, and disclosures explaining that the loan stays in the seller's name. The exact package depends on your state and your closer, so have a title company or real estate attorney prepare or review it.

What happens after a subject-to deal closes?

The work continues. Someone has to make the mortgage payment on time every month, keep insurance and taxes current, track escrow changes and keep records the seller can see. Many investors set up a third-party loan servicer and calendar every recurring obligation on day one.

The bottom line

Subject-to can be one of the most powerful tools in a creative-finance investor's kit, but only if the details are handled. Verify the loan, line up a closer who does these deals, get insurance right, and set up servicing before the first payment is due. Use this subject-to closing checklist on every deal, or hand the file to a TC who will run it for you.
