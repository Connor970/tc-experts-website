Subject-To Deal Checklist: How to Close a Subject-To Transaction Cleanly

November 23, 2026 · 7 min read

A subject-to deal can be one of the best tools in a creative-finance investor's kit. You take title to the property while the seller's existing mortgage stays in place, and you make the payments going forward. But subject-to files have more moving parts than a cash closing, and the mistakes tend to show up months later. A clear subject-to deal checklist keeps every document, deadline and handoff accounted for, from the first seller call to the first payment you make after closing.

Below is the checklist we use as a starting point on creative-finance files. Every deal and every state is different, so treat it as a framework and have a local real estate attorney draft or review your subject-to documents.

Why subject-to deals need their own checklist

On a standard cash purchase, the seller's loan gets paid off at closing and that is the end of it. On a subject-to deal, the loan keeps living after closing. That changes what you need to verify and track:

  • The loan stays in the seller's name. You need accurate, verified loan details, not just what the seller remembers.
  • The seller stays involved. Authorizations, insurance changes and communication with the servicer all depend on the seller's cooperation.
  • Not every closer will touch it. Some title companies decline subject-to transactions or have extra requirements.
  • Post-closing matters as much as closing. Missed payments, lapsed insurance or a servicer notice nobody saw can undo a good deal.

Most mortgages include a due-on-sale clause, which generally gives the lender the right to call the loan due if the property is transferred. How lenders respond varies, and the rules and exceptions are not something to guess at. Make sure the seller understands the structure in writing, and talk through the risk with your attorney before you sign.

Phase 1: Before you sign the contract

Get the real loan numbers

Ask the seller for a recent mortgage statement for every loan on the property, including any second mortgage or HELOC. You want the servicer name, loan number, current balance, interest rate, monthly payment and what that payment includes (principal and interest only, or taxes and insurance through escrow).

Check whether the loan is current

If the seller is behind, you need to know exactly how far. Request a reinstatement quote from the servicer so you know the amount needed to bring the loan current, and whether a foreclosure has been filed. A seller's estimate is not good enough when a sale date may be on the calendar.

Look at the rest of the title picture

The mortgage is only one piece. Judgments, tax liens, unpaid property taxes, code violations and HOA balances can all follow the property. A quick preliminary title search before you commit helps you price the deal correctly.

Line up your closer early

Call your title company or closing attorney before you send the contract and confirm they close subject-to deals. Ask what they need from you and the seller. If you are still building your bench, our guide to finding an investor-friendly title company covers the questions to ask.

Phase 2: Under contract

Once the purchase agreement is signed, the clock starts. This is where a disciplined checklist earns its keep.

  1. Open title and send the executed contract, any addenda and the seller's contact information the same day.
  2. Collect a signed authorization from the seller allowing you or your team to speak with the servicer about the loan. Without it, the servicer generally will not discuss the account with you.
  3. Verify the loan directly with the servicer. Confirm the balance, payment amount, due date, escrow status and whether the account is current.
  4. Order the reinstatement quote if the loan is behind, and note its good-through date so it does not expire before closing.
  5. Track earnest money delivery and confirm the title company received it.
  6. Request HOA documents and balances if the property is in an association.
  7. Schedule inspections and access within your contract's timelines.
  8. Review the title commitment as soon as it arrives and start curative work on anything that needs to be cleared.

Quick tip: Put every date in one place: the contract deadlines, the reinstatement good-through date, the loan's next due date and the closing date. On subject-to files, a missed mortgage payment during escrow is one of the easiest problems to prevent and one of the most painful to fix.

Phase 3: Insurance and escrow details

Update the insurance

The existing homeowner's policy was written for an owner-occupant. Once you take title, that coverage may no longer fit how the property is used. Talk to an insurance agent who works with investors about the right policy, and about how the lender should be listed so the servicer's records stay in order. Have the new coverage in place on or before closing.

Decide how to handle the escrow account

If the loan has an escrow account for taxes and insurance, there is usually a balance sitting in it. Decide in your contract whether that balance is credited to the seller at closing or stays with the loan, and make sure the settlement statement reflects that decision.

Plan for property taxes

If taxes are not escrowed, find out what is owed and when the next bill is due. Prorations should show on the settlement statement, but the next payment after closing is on you.

Phase 4: Closing day

  • Review the settlement statement line by line. Confirm any reinstatement amount, seller credits, escrow balance treatment and your cash to close.
  • Confirm all subject-to documents prepared or reviewed by your attorney are in the closing package and signed.
  • Confirm the deed and the vesting, especially if you are taking title in an LLC or trust.
  • Arrange remote or mobile signings if the seller has already moved or lives out of state.
  • Get copies of everything the seller signed, and store them with your loan verification records.

Phase 5: After closing

This is the part of the subject-to checklist that most investors underestimate. The deal is closed, but the loan still needs to be managed for as long as you own the property.

  1. Confirm the deed was recorded and get a recorded copy from the title company.
  2. Set up the mortgage payment so it goes out on time every month. Many investors use automatic payments or a third-party loan servicing company to create a clean payment record.
  3. Make sure servicer mail reaches you. Statements and notices still go to the seller's name. Have a plan, agreed in writing with the seller, for how you will see them.
  4. Transfer utilities and confirm the seller has fully vacated.
  5. Calendar insurance renewals and tax due dates so nothing lapses.
  6. Keep the seller informed. A seller who knows their loan is being paid is far less likely to panic and call the lender.

Wholesaling a subject-to deal

Some investors lock up subject-to deals and assign them to another creative-finance buyer instead of keeping them. If you do, everything above still applies, plus the assignment itself. Confirm your contract allows assignment, check that your title company will close an assigned subject-to file, and make sure the end buyer receives the full loan verification package.

Common subject-to mistakes to avoid

  • Relying on the seller's numbers instead of verifying the loan with the servicer.
  • Letting the reinstatement quote expire before closing.
  • Missing a payment during escrow because nobody owned the due date.
  • Leaving the old insurance in place without confirming it still covers the property.
  • Forgetting a second lien or HELOC that never came up in conversation.
  • Using generic forms instead of documents prepared or reviewed by an attorney in the property's state.

Frequently asked questions

What documents do I need to close a subject-to deal?

At a minimum you want the signed purchase agreement with subject-to terms, a recent mortgage statement, a signed authorization from the seller allowing you to speak with the lender, a reinstatement quote if the loan is behind, updated insurance, the title commitment and the deed. Many investors also use additional disclosures and agreements prepared by a local real estate attorney.

Will every title company close a subject-to deal?

No. Some title companies have policies against closing transactions where the existing loan stays in place, or have specific requirements for them. Ask before you send the contract, and work with a title company or attorney that regularly closes creative-finance deals.

Can a transaction coordinator handle a subject-to deal?

Yes. A TC who works creative-finance files can track the loan verification, seller authorizations, insurance changes, title requirements and closing deadlines, and keep the seller, title company and investor updated. The TC does not give legal advice, so your attorney still drafts or reviews the subject-to documents.

The bottom line

A subject-to deal is only as good as the details behind it. Verify the loan, get the seller's authorizations in writing, fix the insurance, line up a closer who knows creative finance, and keep managing the loan after closing.
