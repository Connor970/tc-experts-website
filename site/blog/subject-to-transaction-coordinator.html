Subject-To Transaction Coordinator: What a TC Handles on a Sub-To Deal

November 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Subject-to deals can be some of the best opportunities in creative finance, and some of the easiest to get wrong. You are taking title while the seller's existing mortgage stays in place, which means more documents, more parties and more things that have to keep working long after closing day. A subject-to transaction coordinator keeps all of those moving pieces organized so you can focus on finding and negotiating the next deal.

Here is what makes sub-to files different, what a TC handles from contract to closing, what happens after closing, and how to decide whether to hand the file off.

Why subject-to deals need more coordination than a standard closing

On a typical cash or financed purchase, the seller's loan is paid off at closing and that chapter ends. On a subject-to deal, the loan stays in the seller's name and the buyer takes over making the payments. That one difference adds a long list of moving parts:

  • Loan information you cannot skip. You need the current balance, payment amount, interest rate, escrow details and whether the loan is current or behind.
  • Seller authorizations. Lenders generally will not talk to you about the seller's loan without written permission from the seller.
  • Insurance changes. The property's insurance has to reflect the new owner while the lender stays listed correctly on the policy.
  • Extra documents. Sub-to closings often include disclosures and agreements beyond a standard purchase, prepared by your attorney or title company.
  • Life after closing. Payments, escrow and communication with the seller continue for as long as the loan is in place.

Every one of those items has an owner, a deadline and a follow-up. Miss one and the deal can stall, or worse, cause problems months later.

What a subject-to transaction coordinator handles before closing

Collecting the seller's loan details

The first job is getting a clear picture of the existing loan. A TC requests the most recent mortgage statement, the loan number, the servicer's contact information and the escrow breakdown for taxes and insurance. If the seller has a second mortgage, a HELOC or other liens, those get tracked too.

Getting signed authorizations

Your attorney or title company will usually provide an authorization form that lets the servicer release loan information to you or your team. A TC gets it signed early, sends it to the servicer and follows up until the servicer confirms it is on file. This step alone can take days if nobody is chasing it.

Ordering reinstatement or payoff figures

Many sub-to sellers are behind on payments or facing foreclosure. If so, the TC orders a reinstatement quote from the servicer so everyone knows exactly what it takes to bring the loan current at closing. If there is a foreclosure sale date, it goes at the top of the timeline and every other deadline works backward from it.

Opening title and confirming the closer

Not every title company closes subject-to transactions. A TC confirms the closer is comfortable with the structure before the contract goes over, then opens title, sends the contract and tracks the title commitment. Liens, judgments, unpaid taxes or HOA balances get flagged as soon as they show up. Coordinating insurance

Insurance is one of the most common loose ends on sub-to deals. Investors often set up a new policy or adjust the existing one so the buyer is protected and the lender remains listed as the mortgagee. The right setup depends on the deal and your insurance agent's advice. The TC's job is to make sure the agent has what they need, the policy is bound before closing and the declarations page is in the file.

Tracking the document package

Your attorney or title company prepares the closing documents. Depending on the deal and the state, that may include the deed, seller disclosures and acknowledgments about the existing loan, and agreements covering how payments will be handled. A TC keeps a checklist of every document the closer requires, collects what is needed from the seller and buyer, and makes sure nothing is missing on signing day.

Scheduling the signing

Sellers on sub-to deals are sometimes out of state, behind on bills or dealing with a life change like a divorce, job move or inheritance. A TC schedules the signing, arranges a mobile notary or remote signing when the closer allows it, and keeps the seller informed so there are no last-minute surprises.

Quick tip: Ask for the seller's mortgage statement and signed authorization on the same day the contract is signed. Almost every other step on a sub-to file depends on having accurate loan information, so getting it early saves days.

What happens after closing on a subject-to deal

A standard transaction is basically done once the deed records. A subject-to deal is just getting started. After closing, the TC can help with:

  • Confirming the deed recorded and getting a copy into your file.
  • Setting up payments, whether you pay the servicer directly or use a loan servicing company, and confirming the first payment posts on time.
  • Tracking escrow changes when tax or insurance amounts shift and the monthly payment changes.
  • Organizing the closing package so the settlement statement, recorded deed, authorizations, insurance and loan statements are easy to find later.
  • Handling the handoff to your property manager, tenant or end buyer if you plan to rent, wrap or resell.

Late or missed payments are the fastest way to damage the relationship with your seller and put the deal at risk. A clean handoff and a confirmed first payment matter as much as anything that happens before closing.

The due-on-sale clause: what your TC can and cannot do

Most mortgages include a due-on-sale clause, which generally gives the lender the right to require the loan be paid in full if the property is transferred. That risk is part of every subject-to deal, and how you address it, disclose it and structure around it is a legal question.

A transaction coordinator does not give legal advice. What a TC can do is make sure the documents your attorney prepares are signed, the seller's acknowledgments are in the file, and every party has copies of what they agreed to. Rules for creative-finance transactions also vary by state, so work with a real estate attorney or title company that knows the rules where the property is located.

Subject-to, seller finance and wraps: same idea, different paperwork

Creative-finance investors often mix structures. A deal might be part subject-to and part seller carry, or you might buy subject-to and then sell on a wrap. Each structure adds its own documents, such as a promissory note and deed of trust or mortgage for the seller-financed portion, and its own payment schedule to track.

A TC who works creative-finance files keeps each layer straight: which loan is being taken over, which note the seller is carrying, which payments go where and when. That organization is what lets you stack structures without losing track of obligations.

Should you use a transaction coordinator on your sub-to deals?

If you close one sub-to deal a year and enjoy the paperwork, you may be fine handling it yourself. A TC tends to pay for itself when:

  • You are doing several creative-finance deals at once.
  • Your sellers are behind on payments and timelines are tight.
  • You are buying in markets where you do not have a local team.
  • You would rather spend your time on leads, negotiations and buyers than on servicer follow-ups.

If you also wholesale some of your sub-to leads, the same coordinator can keep those files moving too. Frequently asked questions

What does a transaction coordinator do on a subject-to deal?

A subject-to transaction coordinator collects the seller's loan details and authorizations, opens title, tracks the reinstatement or payoff figures if the loan is behind, coordinates insurance changes, gathers the extra documents your attorney or title company prepares, schedules signing and helps confirm the mortgage payment is set up after closing.

Does a subject-to deal need a title company?

Closing through a title company or real estate attorney is the common approach, because it gives you a title search, a recorded deed and, when available, title insurance. Not every closer handles subject-to transactions, so confirm before you send the contract.

Can a TC give legal advice on the due-on-sale clause?

No. A transaction coordinator manages documents, deadlines and communication. Questions about the due-on-sale clause, disclosures or state rules for creative-finance deals belong with a real estate attorney licensed in the property's state.

The bottom line

Subject-to deals reward investors who stay organized. Loan details, authorizations, reinstatement figures, insurance, closing documents and post-closing payments all have to line up, and each one depends on someone following up. A subject-to transaction coordinator owns that follow-up, so your deals close cleanly and keep performing after the deed records.
