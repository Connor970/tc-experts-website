Why Wholesale Deals Fall Through (and How to Keep Yours Closing)

November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

Getting a property under contract feels like the win. But anyone who has wholesaled for more than a few months knows the contract is only the starting line. Somewhere between signing and closing, a lien shows up, a buyer goes quiet, or a deadline slips by, and the assignment fee you were counting on disappears.

The good news is that most of the reasons why wholesale deals fall through are predictable. They show up over and over, and nearly every one of them can be caught early with the right process. Here are the most common deal killers, what causes them, and how to keep your files moving to the closing table.

Why wholesale deals fall through after the contract is signed

Wholesale deals are more fragile than retail sales for a few simple reasons. Timelines are short. The seller is often dealing with stress, like a pre-foreclosure, an inherited house or a property they cannot afford to fix. The end buyer has not been found yet, or was found quickly. And there are more parties involved: seller, wholesaler, end buyer, title company and sometimes a transactional lender.

Every extra party and every short deadline is another place for the deal to break.

1. Title problems that surface too late

Title is the single biggest source of surprises in wholesaling. Distressed properties tend to carry baggage, and you often do not see it until the title commitment comes back.

What usually shows up

  • Mortgages and payoffs that are higher than the seller told you, sometimes higher than your contract price.
  • Tax liens, judgments and code enforcement liens that have to be paid or released before closing.
  • HOA balances, fines and transfer requirements that take time to get from the association or management company.
  • Heirship and probate issues when the person who signed your contract is not the only person with an interest in the property.
  • Missing signers, like a spouse or co-owner who never agreed to sell.

How to prevent it

Open title the same day the contract is signed, not after you find a buyer. Order payoffs early, ask the seller up front about mortgages, back taxes, HOAs and other owners, and read the title commitment as soon as it lands. A problem found in week one is a negotiation. A problem found the day before closing is usually a dead deal.

If a title issue looks complicated, such as an estate that has not gone through probate, bring in the title company or a real estate attorney in your state early. Rules on probate, heirship and liens vary by state, so get local guidance rather than guessing.

2. The end buyer backs out or never funds

Your assignment fee depends on someone else showing up with the money. When they do not, the deal stalls.

Common reasons buyers walk

  • The repair estimate or ARV did not hold up once they walked the property.
  • They tied up several deals at once and only planned to close one.
  • Their hard money or private lender did not come through in time.
  • They saw a title issue and decided it was not worth the wait.

How to prevent it

Vet buyers before you assign. Ask for proof of funds and a list of recent purchases. Require a meaningful, non-refundable earnest money deposit where your contract and local practice allow it, and make the deposit due quickly. Give buyers accurate information about condition and title so they are not surprised later. And keep a short list of backup buyers for every deal, so a walkaway is a delay rather than a loss.

3. Earnest money is late or never receipted

Earnest money problems are easy to overlook and surprisingly common. Your EMD to the seller might be due within a set number of days. Your end buyer's EMD might be due on a separate schedule. If either one is late, a seller looking for a way out may have one, depending on your contract terms.

How to prevent it: Put every EMD due date on a calendar the day the contract and assignment are signed. Confirm with title that each deposit was actually received, not just sent, and keep the receipt in the file.

4. Missed contract deadlines

Every purchase agreement has dates: inspection or option periods, title objection deadlines, financing contingencies and the closing date itself. Wholesalers juggling several deals at once lose track of them, and a missed date can give the other side leverage or an exit.

How to prevent it: Build a timeline for each file as soon as it is signed. Note who is responsible for each step and set reminders a few days before each deadline, not on the day. If you need more time, ask for a written extension before the deadline passes, not after.

5. The seller gets cold feet

Sellers in wholesale deals are often under pressure, and pressure makes people second-guess. A relative tells them they sold too cheap. Another investor calls with a higher number. They see the assignment fee on a settlement statement and feel taken advantage of. Or they simply stop answering the phone.

How to prevent it

  • Be clear from the start. Explain how the process works, including that you may assign the contract or bring in a partner, in plain language.
  • Stay in touch. A seller who hears from someone regularly is far less likely to drift. Silence breeds doubt.
  • Solve their problem, not just yours. If the seller needs a later move-out date or help clearing out the house, that may matter more to them than price.
  • Consider a double close when you expect the fee to cause friction, so the seller and end buyer are on separate transactions.

6. Title company or paperwork issues

Not every title company is comfortable closing assignments or double closes. Some will not do them at all, and some have extra requirements you only learn about mid-deal. On top of that, small paperwork gaps cause big delays: an unsigned addendum, a missing initial, a contract that does not allow assignment, or a buyer entity name that does not match the assignment agreement.

How to prevent it: Use investor-friendly title companies you have closed with before, and confirm at the start whether they will close an assignment or a double close on this file. Review every document for signatures, initials and correct names before sending it to title. Make sure your purchase contract allows assignment if that is how you plan to close.

Some states also regulate wholesaling, including disclosure or licensing requirements. These rules change and differ by state, so check with a real estate attorney where you do business before you rely on any particular contract or disclosure.

7. Communication breaks down

Underneath almost every failed deal is a moment where someone stopped hearing from someone else. Title was waiting on a document nobody knew about. The buyer assumed the seller had moved out. The seller assumed the closing date had been set. By the time anyone noticed, it was too late.

How to prevent it: Assign one point of contact for each file and send regular status updates to every party, even when the update is "no change." Write down what each party is waiting on and who owes it.

How a transaction coordinator keeps wholesale deals from falling through

Look at the list above and you will notice something. Almost none of these problems are about finding deals or negotiating. They are about follow-through: opening title, tracking dates, confirming deposits, reviewing paperwork and keeping everyone talking. That is exactly the work a transaction coordinator does.

A TC who works with investors opens title the day you hand off the file, orders payoffs and HOA information, tracks every deadline, confirms earnest money, and keeps the seller, buyer and title company updated. When something starts to go wrong, you hear about it early, while there is still time to fix it. For wholesalers: on most wholesale deals, the TC fee is paid by the end buyer at closing. That means the help that keeps your deals from falling through is typically free to you as the wholesaler.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common reason a wholesale deal falls through?

There is rarely just one. The usual culprits are title problems that surface late, an end buyer who backs out or never sends earnest money, and missed contract deadlines. Most of them can be caught early if title is opened right away and every date is tracked from day one.

What happens to my assignment fee if the end buyer backs out?

Your assignment fee is normally paid at closing, so if the deal does not close, you are not paid. What happens to the end buyer's earnest money depends on the terms of your assignment agreement. If you still have time on your purchase contract, you may be able to assign it to a backup buyer. Have an attorney review your contract language so you know your options before it happens.

Can a transaction coordinator stop a wholesale deal from falling through?

A TC cannot fix a bad deal, but they can prevent most of the avoidable failures: late earnest money, missed deadlines, title issues found too late and parties who go silent. On wholesale deals the TC fee is typically paid by the end buyer at closing, so the wholesaler usually pays nothing out of pocket.

The bottom line

Wholesale deals rarely fall apart for mysterious reasons. They fall apart because title was opened late, a deposit was never confirmed, a deadline slipped or someone stopped communicating.
